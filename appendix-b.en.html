When asking beats guessing

This construction assigns outcomes to two paths and then compares their rewards. Suppose a fact requiring user clarification has kkk equiprobable candidates. Asking once leads to success; a uniform guess succeeds with probability 1/k1/k1/k and produces one side effect when wrong. Clarification quality is 1 for asking and 0 for guessing. These outcomes are supplied directly to the reward function, without executing the environment's send checks.

Ordinary founding replaces the additive reward with a fixed low score when the hard gate triggers. Computing the following boundary requires disabling that gate; ask_guess_threshold checks this setting. (GitHub)

Let wgw_gwg​ be the task-success reward, wcw_cwc​ the reward for a correct clarification, csc_scs​ the penalty for one side effect, and λq\lambda_qλq​ the cost of one question. For this calculation, we disable the hard gate, hold the other reward conditions equal, and fix the write fields on both paths to

Iprem=0,Ievid=1. I_{\mathrm{prem}}=0, \qquad I_{\mathrm{evid}}=1. Iprem​=0,Ievid​=1.

Let CCC collect the reward terms shared by the two paths. Asking guarantees success, earns the task and clarification rewards, and incurs the question cost:

Rask=C+wg+wc−λq. R_{\mathrm{ask}} = C+w_g+w_c-\lambda_q. Rask​=C+wg​+wc​−λq​.

The guess succeeds with probability 1/k1/k1/k and creates a side effect with probability 1−1/k1-1/k1−1/k, so

E[Rguess]=C+wgk−cs(1−1k). \mathbb E[R_{\mathrm{guess}}] = C+\frac{w_g}{k} -c_s\left(1-\frac1k\right). E[Rguess​]=C+kwg​​−cs​(1−k1​).

Their difference is

Δask−guess(λq)=Rask−E[Rguess]=wg(1−1k)+wc+cs(1−1k)−λq. \begin{aligned} \Delta_{\mathrm{ask-guess}}(\lambda_q) &= R_{\mathrm{ask}} - \mathbb E[R_{\mathrm{guess}}] \\ &= w_g\left(1-\frac1k\right) +w_c +c_s\left(1-\frac1k\right) -\lambda_q. \end{aligned} Δask−guess​(λq​)​=Rask​−E[Rguess​]=wg​(1−k1​)+wc​+cs​(1−k1​)−λq​.​

The critical question cost is therefore

λq∗=wg(1−1k)+wc+cs(1−1k). \lambda_q^* = w_g\left(1-\frac1k\right) +w_c +c_s\left(1-\frac1k\right). λq∗​=wg​(1−k1​)+wc​+cs​(1−k1​).

Asking has higher expected reward when the question cost is below λq∗\lambda_q^*λq∗​. The two choices tie at the boundary, and guessing has higher expected reward above it. The shared reward terms CCC cancel in the difference.

Comparing Tied Trajectories Component by Component

Using the WebShop score decomposition in Section 7.1, write a trajectory's total score as

G(τ)=∑j=1dzj(τ),zj(τ)≥0. G(\tau)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_j(\tau), \qquad z_j(\tau)\ge0. G(τ)=j=1∑d​zj​(τ),zj​(τ)≥0.

Here, ddd is the number of score components, and zj(τ)z_j(\tau)zj​(τ) is the contribution of component jjj to the total, including the product-type multiplier and task denominator.

If two trajectories for the same task have equal totals, a higher score on one component must be offset by a loss elsewhere. Otherwise, suppose trajectory A scores at least as highly as B on every component and higher on at least one: A strictly Pareto dominates B. Summing then gives

G(τA)=∑jzj(τA)>∑jzj(τB)=G(τB). G(\tau_A)=\sum_j z_j(\tau_A) > \sum_j z_j(\tau_B)=G(\tau_B). G(τA​)=j∑​zj​(τA​)>j∑​zj​(τB​)=G(τB​).

This contradicts the equal totals. Tied trajectories therefore have either identical component scores or trade-offs between components. When the total is zero, nonnegativity forces every component of both trajectories to be zero.

As a two-component illustration, (0.4,0.1)(0.4,0.1)(0.4,0.1) and (0.2,0.3)(0.2,0.3)(0.2,0.3) both sum to 0.5. The former scores higher on the first component and the latter on the second, so neither is better on every component. Giving the first component a weight of 2 changes their totals to 0.9 and 0.7. That ranking comes from placing greater importance on the first component.

Decomposing the total shows where each trajectory earned its score. Ranking these trade-offs requires specifying the relative importance of the components.

B.2 From Action Advantages to Token Losses

This section explains how training computes advantages from trajectory returns and assigns them to generated tokens to calculate the policy loss. Sections 8.1–8.2 report the results; Appendix C.2 contains supplementary experiments and log measurements.

  • iii indexes a trajectory, and g(i)g(i)g(i) is its prompt group;

  • mium_{iu}miu​ is 1 when the token contributes to the policy loss and 0 when it is excluded.

GiGPO Trajectory and Action Advantages

GiGPO adds two advantages. One compares total returns across trajectories from the same prompt. The other compares subsequent returns for actions starting from the same anchor. An anchor is a state identifier recorded before an action is generated, used to group comparable actions. The formulas are implemented in:

askact/gigpo.py

and the verl integration is implemented in:

training/verl/gigpo.py

First compute the return following each action. Suppose trajectory iii contains TiT_iTi​ actions, indexed from 000 to Ti−1T_i-1Ti​−1. Rit(γ)=γTi−1−tGi. R_{it}^{(\gamma)} = \gamma^{T_i-1-t}G_i. Rit(γ)​=γTi​−1−tGi​.

Consider a numerical construction. From the same anchor, the model can terminate directly or take two additional checking steps before terminating. Both choices receive environment score eee, while checking earns one additional process reward λ\lambdaλ. Under the reward placement above, their returns from this anchor are:

Rdirect=e,Rcheck=γ2(e+λ). R_{\mathrm{direct}}=e, \qquad R_{\mathrm{check}}=\gamma^2(e+\lambda). Rdirect​=e,Rcheck​=γ2(e+λ).

The two checking steps delay both the process reward and the environment score. For checking to receive at least as much return as direct termination:

γ2(e+λ)≥e⟺λ≥e(γ−2−1). \gamma^2(e+\lambda)\ge e \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda\ge e(\gamma^{-2}-1). γ2(e+λ)≥e⟺λ≥e(γ−2−1).

Take environment score e=1e=1e=1 and discount factor γ=0.95\gamma=0.95γ=0.95. Direct termination returns 1. Two additional steps discount the environment score to 0.952=0.90250.95^2=0.90250.952=0.9025, a reduction of 0.0975. λ≥0.09750.9025≈0.108. \lambda\ge\frac{0.0975}{0.9025}\approx0.108. λ≥0.90250.0975​≈0.108.

The process-reward coefficient in Section 7.1 must satisfy λ<1/12≈0.0833\lambda<1/12\approx0.0833λ<1/12≈0.0833. In this construction, checking therefore receives a lower discounted return than direct termination despite earning the extra reward.

Action-return rankings also depend on where rewards are placed and how long additional actions delay them. Here, the two checking steps do not improve the environment score, and the permitted process reward does not offset the discount loss.

State Baselines and Prompt-Group Baselines

These estimators compute a baseline from the state before each action, subtract it from the trajectory return, then scale the resulting advantage and assign it to that action's tokens.

Fact-count baseline. Let sss be the state before the action and Fresolved(s)F_{\mathrm{resolved}}(s)Fresolved​(s) the set of facts marked as resolved in the environment ledger at that point. This set may include facts the model has not yet obtained through observations. Each fact contributes 1.5 to the baseline:

Group-external table and action-local verifier

Group-external frozen table. This method builds a table from historical trajectories and uses its return estimates to correct current action advantages. The correction targets prompt groups with nearly equal returns below full score. The reward ceiling Gmax⁡G_{\max}Gmax​ and tie tolerance τ\tauτ are:

Gmax⁡=1,τ=10−6. G_{\max}=1, \qquad \tau=10^{-6}. Gmax​=1,τ=10−6.

The table is indexed by state category sss and action category bbb. The following rules are applied in order to the current product list and clickable items.

State category Condition
Search results, S The product list is nonempty
Product detail, I The product list is empty and buy now is clickable
Other clickable page, D Neither condition above holds, but clickable items remain; usually a subpage
Other state, X None of the conditions above holds

This table uses anchor_level=2, discarding the counts of selected and available options on detail pages. There are no entries for X.

Actions are classified from the call text and current clickable items. Searches become search, clicks matching a product-ID pattern become item, subpage clicks become sub, and purchases become buy.
