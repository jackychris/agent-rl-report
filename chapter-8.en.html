Chapter 8. Reward Signals in Group-Relative Training

8.1 Group Advantages and Behavioral Differences

When errors remain, do the trajectories sampled for a task provide GRPO with a signal for improvement? We group saved trajectories by task, compare returns and actions, and calculate advantages. Different procedures receiving the same score and repetition of one procedure call for different next checks.

Advantage calculation

For this audit, we sample eight T=1.0T=1.0T=1.0 rollouts per prompt and recompute standard GRPO advantages from the saved rewards. Each trajectory's advantage is

A=R−Rˉs+10−6. A=\frac{R-\bar R}{s+10^{-6}}. A=s+10−6R−Rˉ​.

Here, RRR is this trajectory's reward, while Rˉ\bar RRˉ and sss are the mean and sample standard deviation of the eight rewards in the group. The 10−610^{-6}10−6 term prevents division by zero. Rewards above the group mean yield positive advantages; rewards below it yield negative advantages.

Standard GRPO applies the trajectory's advantage to each generated token that participates in training. Model-generated content enters the loss calculation; observations returned by tools do not.

First consider the ordinary clipped policy term for each token participating in training:

ℓ=−min⁡(ρA, clip⁡(ρ,1−ϵlo,1+ϵhi)A). \ell=-\min\left(\rho A,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{lo}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{hi}})A\right). ℓ=−min(ρA, clip(ρ,1−ϵlo​,1+ϵhi​)A).

AAA is the advantage of the trajectory containing this token. ρ\rhoρ is the ratio of the current-policy probability to the old-policy probability for this token in the same context. Here, the old-policy probability is recomputed by the actor; probabilities recorded by the rollout engine are used separately for discrepancy diagnostics. The two ϵ\epsilonϵ values control the lower and upper clipping bounds for this ratio.

The runs also apply dual clipping to the negative-advantage branch; Appendix B gives the complete formula. The resulting token losses are averaged over all tokens participating in training to obtain the reward-derived policy loss.

When a trajectory's advantage is zero, it no longer supplies an update signal through the policy loss above. KL regularization may still drive model updates, so the reward-derived policy loss and the KL term need to be checked separately. Implementation details are in Appendix B.

Within-group reward differences

To inspect the training signal, we need to examine rewards separately for each prompt. For example, a training batch could contain these three groups of trajectories.

Prompt A: 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2
Prompt B: 10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 10
Prompt C: 18, 18, 18, 18, 18, 18, 18, 18

Rewards across the batch range from 2 to 18, but all eight trajectories for each prompt have the same score. GRPO computes advantages separately within each group, so all advantages in these three groups are zero.

Repeated Actions and Different Choices in Tied Tasks

Substituting a tied group's returns into the formula gives zero advantage for all eight trajectories. The next two examples have returns of 0.5 and 0.1 respectively, but differ in their action variation.

For the first task, all eight runs follow this action chain and score 0.5.

search[long clip-in hair extension natural looking]
→ click[b08h5dcd65]
→ click[natural black #1b]
→ click[buy now]

In all eight trajectories, the model chooses the same product and option. Applying the same deterministic scoring rule to the same task and action chain still gives identical scores. To compare another purchase procedure, first inspect why sampling repeats and whether the available candidates offer different actions. This comparison concerns environment actions, not all generated model tokens.

For the second task, all eight trajectories score 0.1 and share the opening calls.

search[gluten free popcorn]
→ click[b08frlyzfd]
→ click[description]

→ click[b08vy7qx9s]

1 rollout:  click[sea-salt]        → click[buy now]
7 rollouts: click[sweet-and-salty] → click[buy now]

Two flavor choices are already present. The next check is the user request and its scoring components: does this difference deserve a reward difference? The sampling file preserves actions and returns, but not the complete request, so this example does not prescribe which flavor should score higher. Favoring an arbitrary flavor just to break the tie would mistake behavioral variation for task progress.

These tasks present different training problems. Where relevant action differences exist, reward determines how to distinguish them. Where saved actions are identical, another procedure to compare is missing. Task requirements determine which action differences merit reward.

How reward changes affect advantages

If every trajectory in a group receives the same additional reward ccc, the mean reward also increases by ccc, while the standard deviation stays unchanged. Subtracting the mean removes this constant when computing the advantage.

A′=(R+c)−(Rˉ+c)s+10−6=A. A'=\frac{(R+c)-(\bar R+c)}{s+10^{-6}}=A. A′=s+10−6(R+c)−(Rˉ+c)​=A.

Adding or subtracting the same amount from every trajectory's reward therefore leaves the group's advantages unchanged.

For example, adding 0.08 for a visit and subtracting 0.08 for no visit differ only by a uniform deduction.

Visited? Visit bonus No-visit penalty
Yes Original reward + 0.08 Original reward
No Original reward Original reward − 0.08

The right column is always 0.08 below the left. This difference disappears when computing group advantages, so the two reward schemes produce identical advantages for the same trajectories.

Increasing the reward coefficient does not necessarily increase the advantage. Suppose all trajectories in a group have the same terminal score ccc and differ only in their process score qqq. Their total reward is

R=c+λq,λ>0. R=c+\lambda q, \qquad \lambda>0. R=c+λq,λ>0.

Increasing λ\lambdaλ scales both the reward differences and their standard deviation. Substituting into the advantage formula gives

A=q−qˉsq+10−6/λ, A=\frac{q-\bar q}{s_q+10^{-6}/\lambda}, A=sq​+10−6/λq−qˉ​​,

where qˉ\bar qqˉ​ and sqs_qsq​ are the mean and sample standard deviation of the process scores in the group. When the numerical stabilizer has a negligible effect, increasing the positive coefficient barely changes the advantage.

If all eight trajectories visit the page and receive the same visit bonus, that bonus becomes a constant within the group. Increasing it further does not change the advantages. If the terminal scores are also identical, all eight advantages are zero.

When both terminal and process scores vary within the group, adjusting the coefficient changes their relative influence on the total reward. The full derivation is in Appendix B.

Breaking the total reward into component scores shows what each of two tied trajectories gets right. For example, one selects the correct color but exceeds the budget; the other stays within budget but selects the wrong color. If color and price are each worth one point, the trajectories receive the same total score.

Making training favor one of these choices requires changing the scoring rule. Increasing the weight on color, for example, gives the first trajectory a higher score. The new ordering comes from prioritizing color.

Zero-Advantage Training Steps in Ask-or-Act

The preceding results come from post-training sampling. To inspect zero advantages during training, we analyze the Ask-or-Act prompt-group state-baseline run group-phi-k0, using askact_group_phi and c1-train.parquet. The log later contains a separate WebShop run. We first select the Ask-or-Act segment by launch markers, then retain the first record for each training step, obtaining 230 steps.

In 136 of the 230 steps, all advantages and the corresponding policy loss are zero. This occurs for 35 consecutive steps from 130 through 164. Training continues, but these steps receive no policy update signal from the sampled rewards.

Across these 136 steps, the KL loss remains nonzero, with a mean of 0.0827 and a range of 0.0627–0.1026. KL regularization may continue driving model updates, but task reward no longer guides these steps through group advantages. Checking whether training is still learning the target behavior therefore requires inspecting advantages and policy loss separately.

The following experiments try to improve sampling and scoring, testing whether these changes can provide an effective training signal for the target behavior.

GiGPO action advantages

Standard GRPO uses the same advantage for all generated tokens in a trajectory. GiGPO additionally compares actions taken from the same state and computes local advantages from the rewards obtained afterward.

For example, several trajectories for the same task reach the same product page. Some continue reading information, while others buy directly. GiGPO compares these actions and applies each local advantage only to the generated tokens of the corresponding action.

Starting from the current action, we discount subsequent rewards by their distance and sum them to obtain the action's discounted return.

Q=r0+γr1+γ2r2+⋯+γnrn. Q=r_0+\gamma r_1+\gamma^2r_2+\cdots+\gamma^n r_n. Q=r0​+γr1​+γ2r2​+⋯+γnrn​.

Here, r0r_0r0​ is the current action's reward, r1r_1r1​ is the next action's reward, and rnr_nrn​ is the final action's reward. We use γ=0.95\gamma=0.95γ=0.95: each additional step before a reward is received multiplies its contribution to the current action by another 0.950.950.95.

The implementation determines whether states match using the current page content and the preceding purchase and subpage-visit counts. Actions are compared only when they share the same prompt, page content, and counts.

In this experiment, we add the trajectory and local advantages directly, without dividing by the standard deviation.

A=(G−Gˉ)+(Q−Qˉ). A=(G-\bar G)+(Q-\bar Q). A=(G−Gˉ)+(Q−Qˉ​).

GGG is the return of the complete trajectory, and Gˉ\bar GGˉ is the mean trajectory return for the same prompt. QQQ is the current action's discounted return, and Qˉ\bar QQˉ​ is the mean discounted return of actions taken under the same prompt and state.

The first term compares complete trajectory outcomes; the second compares subsequent outcomes from the same state.

If a state occurs only once under the same prompt, its discounted return equals the mean for that state, making its local advantage zero. The action is still trained using the trajectory advantage.

This implementation assigns the entire trajectory reward, including the visit bonus, to the final action. Suppose the model has opened a product-detail page and both procedures ultimately buy the same product with terminal score eee.

Buy directly: buy
Visit before buying: open subpage → return to product details → buy

Buying directly receives eee immediately. Visiting first adds a bonus λ\lambdaλ, but the reward arrives two steps later, so its contribution to the current action's discounted return is multiplied by γ\gammaγ twice.

Qbuy directly=e,Qvisit before buying=γ2(e+λ). Q_{\text{buy directly}}=e, \qquad Q_{\text{visit before buying}}=\gamma^2(e+\lambda). Qbuy directly​=e,Qvisit before buying​=γ2(e+λ).

For visiting before purchase to yield a discounted return at least as high as buying directly, the visit bonus must satisfy

λ≥e(γ−2−1). \lambda\ge e(\gamma^{-2}-1). λ≥e(γ−2−1).

When both paths achieve the full score e=1e=1e=1, using γ=0.95\gamma=0.95γ=0.95 requires a visit bonus of at least approximately 0.108. Section 7.1 limits the visit bonus to below 0.0833 to preserve the priority of full-score purchases. The default value of 0.08 gives

Qvisit before buying=0.952×1.08=0.9747,Qbuy directly=1. Q_{\text{visit before buying}}=0.95^2\times1.08=0.9747, \qquad Q_{\text{buy directly}}=1. Qvisit before buying​=0.952×1.08=0.9747,Qbuy directly​=1.

The local-return difference favors direct purchase by 0.0253, but GiGPO also includes the episode-level advantage. For a two-trajectory comparison sharing this state, the episode-return difference is +0.08 in favor of visiting. The difference between the combined advantages is

ΔA=0.08+(0.9747−1)=0.0547. \Delta A=0.08+(0.9747-1)=0.0547. ΔA=0.08+(0.9747−1)=0.0547.

In a simplified example where both the prompt group and the shared-state group contain only these two paths, each once, the combined advantages assigned to visiting and direct purchase are +0.02735 and −0.02735, respectively. Discounting reduces the visit preference but does not reverse the combined signal in this example.

Scoring with Group-External Historical Returns

Trajectories for the same task may take different actions but receive the same final reward. To give these actions different scores, we build a lookup table from another 7,900 training trajectories.

The table contains nine observed state–action combinations. Each entry averages the terminal returns associated with the corresponding action samples; one trajectory can contribute multiple samples. During training, a matching state and action can receive a score from this table instead of relying only on reward differences within the current prompt group. The classifications are given in Appendix B.2, under “Group-External Tables and Action-Level Verifiers.”

The table uses text matching to judge whether an option meets the request. After lowercasing the text, an option name with at least two characters is classified as a match if it appears in the user instruction, and as a non-match otherwise. For example, if the instruction contains black, selecting black is classified as a match. This rule does not use the target options in goal_options used by the environment's scorer.

On a product page, matching and non-matching actions receive scores of 0.822049 and 0.662750, respectively. These values come from mean historical terminal returns, so the table assigns a higher score to text-matching options.

When trajectories for the same prompt receive equal rewards below the maximum, we use the lookup table to adjust action advantages.

additional advantage=κ×(action score−mean score for the same state category). \text{additional advantage} =\kappa\times(\text{action score}-\text{mean score for the same state category}). additional advantage=κ×(action score−mean score for the same state category).

The mean counts each action occurrence in the current training batch that meets these conditions and has a table entry. The additional advantage is added to the original GRPO advantage and used to train the generated tokens of the corresponding action.

We compare training without the table (κ=0\kappa=0κ=0) and with it (κ=3\kappa=3κ=3), keeping all other training conditions the same. Both runs train for 75 steps.

For example, if the task requires black, selecting black receives +1+1+1, while selecting white from the page receives −1-1−1. Search and purchase actions are not option selections and receive 000.

State-Baseline Comparisons in Ask-or-Act

We now return to Ask-or-Act. The state baseline uses facts resolved in the environment ledger before the action and the preceding action history. Subtracting this baseline from the terminal return gives an action advantage.

Section 5.3 compares three random seeds for this method. On both the two-tool and single-tool numerical-decision tests, seeds 1 and 3 complete pre-write lookup on all 60 tasks, while seed 2 reaches 0/60 on both. The same advantage computation produces two opposite action orderings, so each training run needs to be assessed against its intended behavior.

We also fit a linear state baseline using the original-task evaluation of the reweighted-curriculum model in Section 6.3. The data contain 1,331 pre-action state entries, corresponding to 201 states and 13 feature combinations. The script averages terminal returns within each feature combination, then fits these 13 combinations by equally weighted least squares, giving

baseline=10.1225−0.2804F−0.8962B+0.9813W. \text{baseline}=10.1225-0.2804F-0.8962B+0.9813W. baseline=10.1225−0.2804F−0.8962B+0.9813W.

Here, FFF counts resolved facts, BBB counts preceding actions with a nonempty blocked_reason, including reads, user interactions, or writes, and WWW counts writes actually completed. All three counts come from the state before the action. The coefficients remain fixed during training, and each action's advantage is computed as follows.

action advantage=terminal return−baseline. \text{action advantage}=\text{terminal return}-\text{baseline}. action advantage=terminal return−baseline.

We run a separate shuffled comparison with this linear baseline. The model still completes most original tasks after state–baseline correspondence is shuffled. This motivates examining how baseline values affect advantage magnitude and sign. The shuffled run trains for 230 steps, whereas the unshuffled linear-baseline probe trains for only 20; these runs do not provide an effect comparison at equal training lengths.

Token length and training weight

The preceding methods change action advantages, while loss aggregation also determines how much weight each trajectory receives in training. The current implementation sums the losses over all participating tokens and divides by their total count. Trajectories with more generated tokens therefore receive greater weight.

For example, consider two trajectories with 100 and 200 participating tokens. Averaging over tokens gives them weights of one third and two thirds, respectively. Averaging within each trajectory first and then averaging the two trajectories gives each a weight of one half.

We first examine how length weighting affects the mean advantage. Let LLL be the number of participating tokens in a trajectory and Aˉ\bar AAˉ their mean advantage. Then

token-weighted mean advantage−trajectory-weighted mean advantage=Cov⁡(L,Aˉ)E[L]. \text{token-weighted mean advantage}-\text{trajectory-weighted mean advantage} =\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(L,\bar A)}{\mathbb E[L]}. token-weighted mean advantage−trajectory-weighted mean advantage=E[L]Cov(L,Aˉ)​.

Here, Cov⁡(L,Aˉ)\operatorname{Cov}(L,\bar A)Cov(L,Aˉ) measures how trajectory length and mean advantage vary together, and E[L]\mathbb E[L]E[L] is the mean length. Token averaging produces a larger value when longer trajectories have higher advantages, and a smaller value when they have lower advantages. This formula describes the change in mean advantage; the actual gradient also depends on token probabilities and their derivatives.

The handwritten-state-baseline run for seed 3 (seed value 20260813) has 230 log entries with length statistics. The early analysis selected the first 83 without documenting why it chose that cutoff. We retain this log prefix to describe length weighting: the ratio of the longest to the shortest nonempty trainable-token response length ranges from 1.90 to 9.18, with a median of 2.49.

The log also retains trajectory-averaged and token-averaged advantages. Their difference (token average minus trajectory average) is positive in 42 log entries and negative in 41, with a mean of approximately −0.00301. Length weighting changes the mean advantage, but not in a consistent direction. The difference can be recomputed directly from these two means; identifying which action drives a parameter change additionally requires the probability ratios, clipping, and token-level gradients.

These experiments connect intermediate scores to actual advantages and test behavior. Combining GiGPO's local and episode terms gives the complete advantage assigned to an action. Comparisons on fixed task sets show that both external-table and correct-answer scoring improve option matching on the control model's intermediate tied tasks, with no clear overall improvement.

In the training examined here, scoring can directly encourage a target action once that action has been sampled. Advantage computation and loss weighting determine how the score enters the update; test tasks show whether the trained model performs the target action more reliably.
