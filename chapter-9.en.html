Chapter 9. Related Work

9.1 Rewards, Tools, and Information Use

Related research examines how to train models to call tools, whether those calls are necessary, and whether the returned information is used. This section introduces existing methods around these questions and draws on the preceding experiments to discuss how they evaluate intermediate actions.

Learning Generalizable Behaviors for Terminal Agents compares equally sized SFT datasets with different skill coverage. The models achieve similar scores after SFT, but the model trained on more diverse demonstrations generalizes better after RL. The authors propose that pre-training and SFT supply basic skills, while RL mainly changes how the model selects and combines them. This relates to our comparison of training routes: similar task scores at the starting checkpoints can lead to different behaviors after further training.

The paper also compares two forms of behavioral feedback. Rewarding verification before completion increases verification frequency without a noticeable improvement in overall performance; penalizing repeated operations both reduces repetition and improves performance. Its verification metric requires only a read-only inspection, such as ls, cat, or grep, among up to three commands immediately before completion. It does not check whether the model uses the returned information to revise its work. This experiment directly relates to our WebShop retrieval-reward experiment. Both observe an increase in the rewarded action. Our separately run text conditions additionally compare saved purchase fields; they do not isolate a fixed-prefix content effect.

Outcome rewards

Search-R1 allows the model to search repeatedly while answering a question and rewards the correctness of the final answer. ReTool lets the model call a code interpreter during mathematical reasoning. It likewise scores the final answer, without a separate reward for code executability. Both improve problem-solving performance with tool calls through outcome rewards. In light of this report's experiments, the more specific questions are which behavioral changes account for these improvements, and whether the model still calls tools at appropriate times and uses the returned information when task requirements change.

GRPO is Secretly a Process Reward Model explains how outcome rewards participate in credit assignment for intermediate steps. When trajectories in a group share identical generated prefixes, the authors use their final returns to construct implicit process rewards and prove that the corresponding objective is equivalent to the GRPO objective. The derivation uses token-level loss aggregation and assumes one update per batch. This makes it useful to ask what an explicit process reward distinguishes beyond the existing signal. In this report, we check whether the added scores distinguish the target behavior and whether those distinctions actually affect training.

Ng, Harada, and Russell study how to add intermediate rewards while preserving the original task's optimal policies. They assign a potential value Φ(s)\Phi(s)Φ(s) to each state and define the additional reward for moving from sss to s′s's′ as

F(s,a,s′)=γΦ(s′)−Φ(s), F(s,a,s')=\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s), F(s,a,s′)=γΦ(s′)−Φ(s),

where γ\gammaγ is the discount factor. Under the theorem's conditions, this reward shaping preserves optimal policies.

The Dark Room in the Reward Channel examines how these additional rewards affect updates in GRPO. When task rewards are identical within a group and all variation comes from shaping rewards, division by the group standard deviation can cancel the scaling introduced by the shaping coefficient. Reducing that coefficient may therefore leave its influence on training undiminished. Chapter 8 of this report likewise examines advantage computation to determine when reward coefficients change update strength.

Process rewards

The WebShop experiment in this report uses a simple process reward: the model receives an additional reward for visiting a designated product subpage before purchase. The score is based on the visit itself. Other process-reward methods also evaluate whether the right tools are selected and whether their calling order meets the task requirements.

RLTR separates the training of tool calling from final-answer generation. It uses another LLM to judge whether the tool calls are complete, then adds rule-based penalties for repeated and erroneous calls to train the tool-calling model. PROVE uses programmatic checks to assess whether calls execute and whether the required steps follow the dependency order in a reference workflow. Tool names and argument values receive separate scores, and calls exceeding a task-specific budget are penalized. These methods incorporate concrete execution requirements into the reward. Ask-or-Act checks action conditions using the fact ledger; Chapter 6 uses a separate trajectory check for the actual passing of returned identifiers. We additionally change which tool provides the information to test whether trained models adapt their execution to the new task requirements.

Process scores can also be learned from model–environment interactions. Choudhury's AgentPRM collects trajectories across repeated runs and computes training targets from the observed returns following the same state and action. A model then learns to predict the subsequent return of that action. This scoring model is used to guide policy training.

Xi et al.'s AgentPRM learns both the value of each step and the value change between adjacent steps to assess the progress made by the current action. It combines observed trajectory rewards with model predictions, using TD and GAE to construct training targets and reduce the sampling cost of running additional continuations to completion from each intermediate state. Both approaches turn interaction outcomes into intermediate-step scores based on subsequent returns, rather than assigning a predetermined score to a particular tool call.

For tasks whose answers are difficult to verify directly, Kimi K2 uses a model as a judge, comparing responses in pairs against explicit rubrics to generate preference signals. During training, it continually updates this judge using the current policy's rollouts on verifiable tasks, teaching it which responses actually produced correct results. This design combines flexible model judgments with verifiable feedback. It also suggests that examining a reward source requires attention to both the judge's criteria and how its judgments are recalibrated.

Tool value

The Tool-Overuse Illusion finds that rewarding only final-answer correctness can lead models to progressively increase unnecessary tool calls. Adding tool efficiency to the reward reduces calls while preserving accuracy. OTC-PO likewise encourages correct answers with fewer tool calls. It measures tool-use efficiency by dividing the total number of correct answers by the total number of tool calls across the test set. These studies examine the tool-call cost of completing a task. Our WebShop experiments separately count rewarded visits, purchase outcomes, and attribute scores of purchased products.

To Call or Not to Call compares scores with and without tool use for the same model on the same task, treating the difference as the benefit of the tool. It distinguishes a model needing help from a particular tool being helpful. Poor performance without external help indicates need; an improvement after tool use indicates that the tool is useful to that model. Tool value therefore depends on the particular model and task.

ToolVision uses this comparison to train visual tool use. Before reinforcement learning, the authors evaluate the SFT model with tools disabled and enabled, selecting questions on which direct answering is unreliable but tool-using trajectories can reach the correct answer. On these questions, the model receives an additional reward when it uses a tool, answers correctly, and encounters no tool-execution errors. The questions eligible for this bonus are determined by the pre-training assessment and remain fixed during RL.

Search-G1 separately tests whether the model can answer correctly without retrieval and whether its answer changes after retrieved observations are removed. The removal preserves the original question, search history, and generated reasoning, after which the model answers again. The authors use these test outcomes to train two predictors that read the model's hidden states, estimating retrieval necessity and answer reliance on retrieved content. For correct searched answers, the two estimates jointly determine the additional retrieval reward. During training, the authors periodically repeat the tests with the updated model and refit the predictors.

SMART uses supervised fine-tuning to teach models when tools are needed. The authors divide each task into steps and use GPT-4o, guided by answers from the source data, to label which steps can be solved through direct reasoning and which require tools, then construct complete demonstrations. Each step includes a rationale explaining why existing knowledge is sufficient or why external help is needed. For example, when a user's preferences are unspecified, the demonstration uses AskUser to ask the user. The model learns both how to call tools and when to choose to call them.

IGPO computes the model's mean token log-probability of the reference answer before and after each interaction turn, using the increase as that turn's reward. Even if the final answer is wrong, an intermediate turn can receive positive feedback when it makes the reference answer more likely. TIPS takes a similar approach, using a frozen model snapshot to compute changes in correct-answer log-probability and periodically refreshing that snapshot. Both methods use changes in correct-answer probability to measure the value of an interaction turn. Our report compares purchase fields across separate tool-observation treatment runs. Those measurements describe responses under the protocol in §7.1, rather than supplying intermediate training rewards.

Use of retrieved content

RAGChecker breaks the model response and reference answer into individually checkable factual claims, then compares them with the retrieved text. It separately measures how much reference-answer information was retrieved and how much of the retrieved correct information appears in the model response. This locates omissions in retrieval or answer generation. It also checks whether incorrect response claims are supported by the retrieved text, distinguishing adoption of noise in the text from errors generated outside it.

ToolFailBench makes mock tools return values that differ from values the model may be familiar with, then checks whether the final answer adopts the returned value. A response is labeled as result ignoring when the model has called the tool but omits the required returned value. The evaluation also records failures such as skipping a required tool or fabricating returned information, and uses tasks requiring no tool to test overuse. Its target is whether the answer follows the tool-returned information.

HERALD tests whether a reward function accepts problematic citations. The authors keep the answer and search history for a task unchanged, replace a citation with a real corpus passage that was never returned to the model, and recompute the reward. An edited trajectory receiving an equal or higher score exposes a reward loophole. Strengthening the check that cited passages must appear in prior retrieval results eliminated the citation-replacement attacks observed in this test set.

Proof-of-Use uses evidence checks for training. Tools assign identifiers to returned content, and the model judges its helpfulness and cites the corresponding identifiers at the next step. In addition to checking citation validity, training replaces some evidence and measures whether the probability of a helpfulness judgment changes accordingly. A judge model also checks whether the final answer is supported by the cited evidence. Citation records, responses to content changes, and answer support thus jointly contribute to scoring.

AttriGuard uses observation interventions to defend against indirect prompt injection. Before executing a proposed tool call, it preserves the original action history, rewrites instruction-like expressions in tool-returned text into more neutral language, and asks the same model to propose the next call again. For example, “You should send the report” becomes “The text states that the report should be sent.” Rewritten observations accumulate for subsequent checks. The system compares the two proposals and blocks calls that fail its function-name and argument-consistency checks.

AttriGuard preserves action history to control pre-intervention conditions. Our WebShop conditions instead run independently from the initial page, and text treatment may repeat. The protocols differ; Section 7.2 therefore reports changes in purchase fields, action sequences, and designated targets together.

These works offer different ways to check information use. Comparing answers with retrieved text claim by claim can locate omissions and incorrect citations. Replacing returned content and rerunning the model reveals whether its decision changes. Reading these behavioral results alongside task outcomes helps identify what an information-use reward actually changes.

9.2 Sampling, Advantage Estimation, and Model Updates

The experiments in Chapter 8 found that equal-return trajectories may contain different procedures or repeat the same action sequence. These situations call for different interventions. We first review methods for handling equal-return groups, then discuss how to generate new procedures and assign rewards to specific actions.

Sample counts and equal-return groups

The U-statistic analysis of GRPO studies how many responses to sample per prompt. The authors express the GRPO policy-gradient estimator as a U-statistic, analyze how estimation error varies with group size, and derive a rule for choosing that size. This analysis examines how increasing the sample count affects estimation precision. Our report additionally examines sample content, asking whether responses contain different procedures and whether the reward distinguishes them.

DAPO Dynamic Sampling filters groups whose answers are all correct or all wrong and continues sampling until enough tasks have both correct and incorrect answers. Each update therefore uses groups with return variation, at the cost of a variable sampling workload per batch.

Query Recycling keeps tasks that currently lack return variation in the sampling pool for later attempts. Tasks that have supplied an effective comparison and been used for an update are removed from the current pool. As the model changes, the same task may later yield both correct and incorrect answers and provide a training signal. The method changes when a task is sampled again.

AVSPO retains equal-return groups and adds synthetic reward values when computing their mean and standard deviation. These values have no corresponding model responses and participate only in advantage calculation. For all-correct groups, lower synthetic rewards give the real responses positive advantages; for all-wrong groups, positive synthetic rewards give them negative advantages. Equal-return responses can thus participate in updates, but every real response within the group still receives the same advantage and is jointly encouraged or penalized.

CIGPO reports a HotpotQA control experiment in which GRPO training increasingly produces format errors, until all trajectories receive a −2.0 format penalty and group advantages become zero. CIGPO adds a reward after each evidence-reading turn, measuring information gain through the change in a frozen reference model's log probability of the correct answer before and after reading. Different evidence-reading sequences can therefore receive different rewards even when their terminal scores are equal.

Trying different actions from the same history

When the model repeatedly generates the same action sequence, it also needs to try other procedures. The following three methods construct different next actions from the same interaction history for training to compare.

PORTool lets the model try different tool calls after the same interaction history and continue each branch to completion. These trajectories form a tree, with shared steps forming the trunk and different calls forming forks. The method scores actions at each fork using subsequent answer correctness together with tool-call formatting and execution feedback, then compares their advantages.

OVCSD selects a state reached along a failed trajectory and lets the same model try again with additional skill guidance. The guided model acts as the teacher, and only continuations that complete the task are used for training. At the first action where the teacher and original trajectory diverge, their preceding interaction histories are identical. Training increases the probability of the teacher action and decreases that of the original action at this point. The teacher's subsequent completion steps are distilled separately, teaching the model to continue along the new path.

Agentic Critical Training (ACT) takes the context at a step in an expert demonstration, asks the initial model to generate alternative actions, and pairs each candidate that differs from the expert action with that action. Training first asks the model to select the expert action from the two choices, then trains it to generate actions directly. The preference labels come from expert demonstrations, under the assumption that the initial model's actions are inferior to expert actions on average.

These three methods generate alternative actions for training. Our WebShop text experiment instead runs separate observation-treatment conditions and compares purchase fields. It does not directly fix a shared interaction prefix and therefore differs from these local-branching controls.

Computing advantages for individual actions

GiGPO identifies repeated environment states in sampled trajectories and groups the actions taken at those states for comparison. Each action occurrence is scored by the discounted cumulative reward from that step onward. Subtracting the group mean and applying the selected normalization yields a step advantage. Training combines this with the trajectory advantage in a weighted sum, allowing different actions to receive different training signals. These calculations reuse existing trajectories without additional sampling or a learned value model.

RTMC further aggregates repeated outcomes of taking the same action at the same state. Their mean return estimates action value, while the mean return across all action occurrences at that state estimates state value. Subtracting the latter from the former yields the action advantage. State matching uses predefined encoding rules. In coding tasks, for example, the method identifies similar progress from files already inspected and modified, without requiring byte-identical interaction histories. Our group-anchor experiment also estimates a state baseline from trajectories for the same prompt, but uses the current trajectory's subsequent return directly rather than averaging repeated outcomes of the same action.

ProGPO groups actions for comparison only when their preceding interaction histories match exactly. Some steps then have no peers, while other groups contain multiple samples with identical returns. In the paper's training traces, this gives zero peer-comparison advantage to 33.7% of WebShop steps and 44.5% of ALFWorld steps. ProGPO additionally estimates state values before and after an action from sampled outcomes and uses their difference as a progress signal. Action comparison still requires identical histories, while state-value estimation combines history groups of different lengths and semantically similar samples.

AgentPRM trains a process reward model to estimate the prospect of eventual success after an action while also fitting value changes between adjacent steps. The former measures the promise of the current procedure; the latter measures progress made by the step. Training labels combine terminal trajectory rewards with the model's current value predictions, using TD and GAE to propagate terminal feedback to earlier steps. The trained reward model can guide search by choosing which candidate-action branches to continue.

CAPO trains a value model to estimate state values at complete action boundaries and uses them to compute action advantages. All tokens within an action share its advantage. For policy updates, it also combines token probability ratios within each action and corrects for action length, making complete actions the unit of clipping and weighting.

TRIAGE uses an LLM to judge the role an action played during execution. The judge reads nearby interaction records and labels the action as decisive progress, useful exploration, no-progress infrastructure, or regression. Fixed rules then map the label to a reward correction. Useful exploration, for example, receives an additional positive reward, while regressive actions are penalized. The correction is added to the trajectory advantage and applied to the corresponding action tokens. In the paper's experiments, each segment is one environment action, such as a WebShop search or click.

Agent Lightning turns each LLM call into a training sample containing its input, output, and assigned reward. The agent executes tasks while the trainer updates the model from these samples, allowing different agent frameworks to retain their existing call workflows. The paper's initial implementation assigns the terminal return to every call in a trajectory, then uses a single-turn RL algorithm to process the samples. Calls are recorded separately, but each step still receives the same return.

Token losses and gradients

DAPO changes the weights of long and short responses in the loss. Original GRPO first averages token losses within each response, then averages across responses, giving each response equal weight. DAPO averages directly over all valid generated tokens, so longer responses receive greater weight. Even with the same trajectories and advantages, the two algorithms can produce different updates.

GSPO changes the probability ratio used for clipping. It compares the probabilities of the complete response under the new and old policies, then applies length normalization to obtain a ratio shared by the whole response. Clipping acts on this response-level ratio rather than being decided separately for each token. All tokens in a response therefore share the same clipping decision.

Balanced Aggregation addresses weight shifts caused by length differences between positive- and negative-advantage responses. For example, when negative-advantage responses are generally longer, averaging over all tokens increases the weight of the negative side. The method separates responses by advantage sign, computes the mean token loss within each subset, and combines the two means with weights proportional to their response counts. Response counts determine the relative weights of the two sides, while token aggregation is retained within each side.

Token Gradient Cancellation further examines updates to tokens shared across responses. If their gradient vectors and weights are identical, zero-sum group advantages make their gradients cancel. Length scaling and probability-ratio weighting can break this balance, making the shared portion change with whole-response scores. The paper proposes two adjustments. Min-Replace sets the weights at relevant positions to the within-group minimum; Orth-Proj adjusts them so that weighted advantages sum to zero. These adjustments target update drift in shared portions. How much gradient actually cancels also depends on whether the gradient vectors at those positions match.

Probability differences between training and inference

Trajectory generation and model updates typically use different computing frameworks. Even with identical model parameters, the two sides may assign different probabilities to the same token. These differences enter the probability ratios used in training, changing sample weights and clipping decisions.

Deterministic Inference across Tensor Parallel Sizes addresses numerical differences introduced by tensor parallelism. Distributing a model across different numbers of GPUs can change the order of floating-point summation and hence output probabilities. The authors fix accumulation order within and across GPUs and integrate the resulting operators into vLLM and FSDP, obtaining bitwise-identical probabilities between generation and training in their experiments.

QaRL addresses differences between low-precision generation and training. Its training forward pass uses low-precision quantization and matrix multiplication aligned with the generation engine, while the backward pass and master weights retain higher precision. Training thus uses arithmetic closer to actual generation while preserving the speed advantage of low-precision generation.

Diagnosing Training Inference Mismatch in LLM Reinforcement Learning builds VeXact, a generation engine whose probability calculations match FSDP, and uses it as a control to examine common corrections. The authors test training-side recomputation of old-policy probabilities, direct use of generation-side probabilities, importance weighting, and sample filtering. Both large deviations at individual tokens and deviations accumulated across a trajectory affect updates. Configurations addressing both more closely match the VeXact control.

Our report examines sampled trajectories to determine whether training has useful comparisons. These studies further show that how those comparisons enter model updates also affects the outcome. Even with unchanged rewards for the same trajectories, differences in loss aggregation and probability computation can change the direction and strength of training.
